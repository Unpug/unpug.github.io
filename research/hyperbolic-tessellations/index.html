Group Actions and Möbius Symmetries in Hyperbolic Tessellations

Aaditya Bilakanti, Palaash Gang, Thantham Jittham
Mentor: Benjamin Vakil
Advised by Simon Rubinstein-Salzedo
Expository paper, Euler Circle, 2025
Expositio ResearchGate Cite

Triangles have angle sum less than \(\pi\), circles grow exponentially in size with radius, and infinitely many distinct regular tessellations are possible.

Abstract

This expository paper explains tessellations of the hyperbolic plane from a differential–geometric viewpoint. We review the upper half–plane (\(\mathbb{H}\)) and Poincaré disk (\(\mathbb{D}\)) models and the Cayley transform linking them. We prove that orientation–preserving isometries are precisely \(\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R})\) acting by Möbius/Blaschke maps and show these maps are conformal and hyperbolic isometries (hence length– and area–preserving). Using group actions of discrete subgroups (Fuchsian groups), we construct tessellations from fundamental domains; the modular group serves as a guiding example. Via Gauß–Bonnet and reflection/triangle–group constructions we derive the existence criterion for regular \(\{p, q\}\) tilings, namely \(\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} < \frac{1}{2}\), and relate angle defect to area. Throughout the article, we highlight geodesics, curvature \(K \equiv -1\), and the role of isometries in organizing hyperbolic tilings.

Models

Two views of the same plane

Hyperbolic geometry emerged in the nineteenth century from the realization that Euclid’s parallel postulate could be replaced, producing a logically consistent geometry of constant negative curvature. There are many models of the hyperbolic plane. We define two that we use throughout.

The upper half-plane \(\mathbb{H}\) is the set of complex numbers \(z = x + iy\) such that \(\Im(z) > 0\). In the upper half-plane model, hyperbolic geodesics are precisely the vertical Euclidean lines and the Euclidean semicircles whose centers lie on \(\mathbb{R}\). The Poincaré disk model, denoted \(\mathbb{D}\), is the open unit disk in the complex plane. Hyperbolic geodesics in \(\mathbb{D}\) are the Euclidean diameters and circular arcs that meet \(\partial\mathbb{D}\) orthogonally.

\[ ds^2_{\mathbb{H}} = \frac{dx^2 + dy^2}{y^2}, \qquad ds^2_{\mathbb{D}} = \frac{4\,|dz|^2}{(1 - |z|^2)^2} \]

The Cayley map \(\Phi : \mathbb{H} \to \mathbb{D}\) and its inverse are

\[ \Phi(z) = \frac{z - i}{z + i}, \qquad \Phi^{-1}(w) = i\,\frac{1 + w}{1 - w}, \]

and \(\Phi\) is an isometry between the two models.

Symmetry

Möbius transformations

The full isometry group of the hyperbolic plane satisfies \(\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}) \cong \mathrm{PGL}(2, \mathbb{R})\), while the subgroup of orientation-preserving isometries is

\[ \mathrm{Isom}^+(\mathbb{H}) \cong \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R}) / \{\pm I\}. \]

Every orientation-preserving isometry of \(\mathbb{H}\) is given by a fractional linear map, unique up to an overall sign, and every Möbius transformation of the Poincaré disk can be written uniquely as

\[ \varphi(z) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R}), \qquad\qquad f(z) = e^{i\theta}\,\frac{z - a}{1 - \bar{a}z}, \quad a \in \mathbb{D},\ \theta \in \mathbb{R}. \]

These disk Möbius maps are conformal, and the identity \(f^*(ds^2_{\mathbb{D}}) = ds^2_{\mathbb{D}}\) shows \(f\) preserves lengths and areas.

A Fuchsian group \(\Gamma\) is a discrete subgroup \(\Gamma \le \mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{R})\) acting properly discontinuously on \(\mathbb{H}\). A fundamental domain for \(\Gamma\) is a closed region \(D \subset \mathbb{H}\) such that \(\bigcup_{\gamma \in \Gamma} \gamma(D) = \mathbb{H}\), and the interiors of \(D\) and \(\gamma(D)\) are disjoint for \(\gamma \ne e\). The images \(\{\gamma(D) : \gamma \in \Gamma\}\) then tessellate \(\mathbb{H}\).

The subgroup \(\mathrm{PSL}(2, \mathbb{Z})\) generated by \(z \mapsto z + 1\) and \(z \mapsto -1/z\) is a classic Fuchsian group. A standard fundamental domain is

\[ D = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{H} : |z| \ge 1,\ |\Re z| \le \tfrac{1}{2} \right\}. \]

Tessellations

When does a {p, q} tiling exist?

A \(\{p, q\}\) tessellation means that each face is a regular \(p\)-gon and that \(q\) faces meet at a vertex. For a hyperbolic triangle \(\Delta\) with internal angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\), Gauß–Bonnet with \(K \equiv -1\) gives the area as the angle defect, and cutting a polygon into triangles generalizes this:

\[ \mathrm{Area}(\Delta) = \pi - (\alpha + \beta + \gamma), \qquad \mathrm{Area}(P) = (n - 2)\pi - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i. \]

There exists a \(\{p, q\}\) tessellation for a given \(p\) and \(q\) if and only if \(\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} < \frac{1}{2}\). For the forward direction, a hyperbolic \(p\)-gon has positive area with interior angle \(\alpha = \frac{2\pi}{q}\), so

\[ (p - 2)\pi - \frac{2p\pi}{q} > 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} < \frac{1}{2}. \]

For the backward direction, take a hyperbolic triangle \(T\) whose interior angles are \(\pi/p, \pi/q, \pi/2\). The index-2 subgroup \(G^+\) of the reflection group \(G\) acts on \(\mathbb{H}\) with fundamental domain a regular \(p\)-gon whose interior angle is \(2\pi/q\). The orbit of this \(p\)-gon under \(G^+\) is a regular \(\{p, q\}\) tessellation.

Comparison

Three geometries

Hyperbolic geometry is one of the three classical geometries of constant Gaussian curvature alongside Euclidean and Spherical geometry.

EuclideanSphericalHyperbolic
Curvature\(K = 0\)\(K = 1/R^2 > 0\)\(K = -1/R^2 < 0\)
GeodesicsStraight linesGreat circlesVertical lines and semicircles orthogonal to \(y = 0\)
Triangle angle sumExactly \(\pi\)Exceeds \(\pi\)Strictly less than \(\pi\)
Parallels through a pointExactly oneNoneInfinitely many
Circumference \(C(r)\)\(2\pi r\)\(2\pi R \sin(r/R)\)\(2\pi R \sinh(r/R)\)
Area \(A(r)\)\(\pi r^2\)\(2\pi R^2 (1 - \cos(r/R))\)\(2\pi R^2 (\cosh(r/R) - 1)\)

In hyperbolic geometry, both circumference and area grow exponentially with \(r\), a fact directly tied to the richness of possible tessellations. These metric differences have direct combinatorial implications: in the Euclidean plane, regular tessellations exist only for \(\{3, 6\}\), \(\{4, 4\}\), and \(\{6, 3\}\), while in the hyperbolic plane, infinitely many \(\{p, q\}\)-tessellations exist whenever \(\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} < \frac{1}{2}\).

Future directions

Open questions

Despite the extensive development of hyperbolic geometry and tessellation theory, many intriguing open questions remain.

  • Classification of hyperbolic tilings. What broader families of tilings exist beyond the regular and semi-regular cases?
  • Growth rates and combinatorics. How does the number of tiles grow as a function of hyperbolic distance from a fixed point? What combinatorial invariants distinguish different tessellations?
  • Tessellations and topology. What kinds of topological surfaces can be realized as quotients of the hyperbolic plane by tessellations induced from Fuchsian groups?
  • Connections to group theory. How do properties of tessellations reflect properties of the corresponding Fuchsian or triangle groups?
