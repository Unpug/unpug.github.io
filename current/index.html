sparse

spatial ai for robotics. summer 2026 at founders, inc.

a robot doesn't work from a picture. it works in space. it has to know where the floor ends, how far the shelf is, and what is moving.

sparse starts there. we take the messy 3d data that sensors capture and turn it into a structured map of a place. then drones go out and build those maps where work actually happens.

1

point clouds

a depth sensor measures distance along thousands of rays. each ray that hits something leaves one point: a position in space, usually with a color. millions of them together make a point cloud.

it's the closest thing to what a robot's sensors actually see. look at the shadows behind each object: no ray reached there, so as far as the data goes, nothing is there.

2

why points aren't enough

point clouds are hard to learn from. there's no order to them, the number of points changes between frames, and the same surface lands in different places each time it's measured. so a model has no consistent target to predict.

a grid fixes that. the cells stay in the same place, frame after frame.

3

the voxelization pipeline

voxelizing means dividing space into small cubes and asking one question of each: did any points land in here?

that last step is where the name comes from. most of the world is air, so most of a 3d grid is empty. store only the cells that hold something, and a whole building becomes small enough to work with.

4

choosing the resolution

voxel size is a trade. large cells are cheap and show you the shape of a room. small ones show you a handle, a helmet, a hand, but the count grows quickly. halve the size, and a surface takes about four times as many cells.

5

drones

a fixed sensor only sees what it faces. a drone can go and look. it flies over a site and takes in the space under it, and every pass fills in what the last one couldn't see. the holes close, and the map grows into the shape of the building.

6

mapping the work

a building is only half of it. the other half is the work: people walking between stations, forklifts on their routes, the paths everyone repeats through a shift.

a static map shows a robot where the walls are. mapping the workforce shows it how a place is actually used.

7

data generation

robots learn from data, and real industrial data is scarce. you can't stop a factory to collect it.

a structured 3d map changes that, because one capture isn't one example. you can rearrange it, relabel it, and view it from a new angle, and each version is new training data with the answers already known.

point clouds to voxels, voxels to maps, maps to data, data to robots that can work in the real world.
